In August 2025, a report from MIT's Project NANDA put a number on something many IT teams already suspected. Drawing on 150 interviews with leaders, a survey of 350 employees and an analysis of 300 public AI deployments, it found that only about 5% of generative AI pilots were producing rapid revenue gains. Most of the rest had stalled, with little or no measurable effect on profit and loss. A year earlier, Gartner had predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs and unclear business value.

The exact figures are debated. The pattern is familiar. A pilot works in the demo, a few enthusiasts like it, and then it sits: not cancelled, not rolled out.

Pilots rarely stall because the model is not good enough. MIT's researchers pointed to integration instead, meaning tools that do not fit how the work is actually done. In practice, most stalls trace back to one of five gaps.

## Five Reasons Pilots Stall

**No business owner.** The pilot is run by IT or an innovation team. Nobody in the business owns the outcome, so nobody pushes it into daily work.

**No measure of success.** "Let's see how people like it" cannot prove anything. Without a baseline, such as hours spent on a task or turnaround time, the budget conversation has nothing to stand on.

**Demo data instead of real data.** The pilot runs on a tidy folder. Production means duplicates, old versions, scanned PDFs and permissions nobody has reviewed in years.

**Security review at the end.** The pilot is built on a tool nobody reviewed. When it is time to scale, security asks about data flows, access and logging, and the answers are not there. The project restarts or stops.

**Costs that only appear at scale.** Seat licenses across the whole firm, usage-based model charges, integration work and admin time are invisible in a 20-person pilot.

> **A pilot proves that AI can do a task. Production needs proof that the firm can run it.**

## What the Pilots That Make It Do Differently

They pick one workflow with a named owner and a number to move. They run on real data, with real permissions, from the first week. They bring security in at the start instead of at the end. And they price the full rollout before the pilot finishes, so the decision to scale is a decision, not a surprise.

How firms adopt AI matters as much as which tool they pick. In MIT's research, buying from specialized vendors and working with partners succeeded about 67% of the time, while internal builds succeeded only about a third as often.

## Do's

1. **Do pick one workflow with a named business owner** who will use the result every week.
2. **Do measure the baseline before the pilot starts,** such as hours per task, turnaround time or error rate.
3. **Do run the pilot on real sources with real permissions,** not a curated demo folder.
4. **Do involve security in week one,** with the data flows, access model and logging on the table.
5. **Do price the full rollout during the pilot,** including usage, integration and admin time.
6. **Do set a decision date** with clear criteria to scale, change or stop.

## Don'ts

1. **Don't run a pilot without an end date.** An open-ended pilot is how "almost ready" lasts a year.
2. **Don't pilot ten use cases at once.** One that works beats ten that are promising.
3. **Don't build the pilot on a tool security has not reviewed** if you intend to scale it.
4. **Don't judge success by enthusiasm.** Usage and the baseline number decide.

## The Cost of Getting This Wrong

1 - Pilot purgatory. Licenses, staff time and attention keep flowing to something that never reaches the people it was meant for.

2 - Shadow AI fills the gap. When the approved tool never arrives, employees keep using unapproved ones, with no controls at all.

3 - The next proposal is harder. A stalled pilot makes leadership skeptical of the next one, even when it is better planned.

## How To Check Your Pilot

1. **Owner** - Who in the business owns the result, and will they use it every week?
2. **Measure** - What number are we trying to move, and what is it today?
3. **Data** - Does the pilot use the same sources and permissions production will use?
4. **Security** - Has security reviewed the data flows, access and logging?
5. **Cost** - What does the full rollout cost, including usage, integration and admin time?
6. **Decision** - What result would make us stop, and on what date do we decide?
